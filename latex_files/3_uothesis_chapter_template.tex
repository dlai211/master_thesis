\section{The Standard Model of particle physics}
\subsection{Brief Overview of SM particles and interactions} \label{subsec:brief}

The Standard Model of particle physics is a relativistic quantum field theory that describes the elementary particles known to date that constitute matter and the four fundamental forces of nature (except gravity). At the Large Hadron Collider (LHC), the most recent particle discovery was the Higgs boson in 2012, which is responsible for electroweak symmetry breaking and mass generation~\cite{ATLAS:2023oaq}.

This chapter introduces the components of the Standard Model and their significance, while the next section, beyond the Standard Model, explains its limitations. 

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/Standard_Model_of_Elementary_Particles.svg.png}
    \caption{Elementary particles of the Standard Model \protect\cite{WikiSM2018}.}
    \label{fig:example}
\end{figure}


Elementary particles in the Standard Model are described by a quantum field theory, corresponding to the symmetry group
\[
SU(3)_C \times SU(2)_L \times U(1)_Y.
\]


There are two classes of particles in the Standard Model, which are fermions or bosons. Each particle has its own antiparticles: charged particles have antiparticles with opposite charge, while all other properties remain the same~\cite{Griffiths_EPP}~\cite{Thomson_MPP}.

Fermions are the matter particles that have spin $\frac{1}{2}$. In the Standard Model, there are six different fermion flavors (three quark flavors and three lepton flavors), split into three generations, all of which follow Fermi-Dirac statistics. The Pauli Exclusion Principle, which is a direct result of the Fermi-Dirac statistics, states that no two fermions can occupy the same quantum state. This is why an electron will move up to a higher energy state in an atom if all of the lower energy states are already occupied by other electrons.

The six flavors of quarks are up, down, charm, strange, top, and bottom quarks. Organized by generations, the increasing mass from left to right, the quarks are represented as:
\[
\begin{pmatrix} u \\ d \end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix} c \\ s \end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix} t \\ b \end{pmatrix}
\]
The first row of the quarks possesses a fractional charge of $+\frac{2}{3}$; the second row has $-\frac{1}{3}$. Their corresponding antiparticles are
\[
\begin{pmatrix} \bar{u} \\ \bar{d} \end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix} \bar{c} \\ \bar{s} \end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix} \bar{t} \\ \bar{b} \end{pmatrix}
\]
All these quarks have a non-Abelian charge called color charge from the local gauge symmetry $SU(3)_c$ of quantum chromodynamics (QCD). Because of color confinement, the quarks do not appear as free particles but as color-neutral bound states, called hadrons. There are two classes of hadrons: the mesons, consisting of a quark and an antiquark, and the baryons, consisting of three quarks or three antiquarks. Mesons are color neutral because the color of a quark is canceled by the anticolor of the corresponding antiquark, such as the triplets of pions ($\pi^+$, $\pi^0$, $\pi^-$).  The three quarks that make up the baryons have colors, red, green, and blue, which are balanced to give a color-neutral object. The two well-known baryons are the proton ($p$) and the neutron ($n$). Quarks interact through all four fundamental forces. The strong force, mediated by gluons, binds quarks into hadrons; the electromagnetic and weak interactions are responsible for charge and flavor changes; the gravitational force, due to its small magnitude, is irrelevant compared to the other three forces. 

The leptons are the electron, muon, tau, electron neutrino, muon neutrino, and tau neutrino. Organized by generations and increasing mass from left to right, the leptons are: 
\[
\begin{pmatrix} e^- \\ \nu_{e} \end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix} \mu^- \\ \nu_{\mu} \end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix} \tau^- \\ \nu_{\tau} \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The first row of leptons has a charge of -1, and neutrinos are charge-neutral and left-handed. Their corresponding anti-particles are 
\[
\begin{pmatrix} e^+ \\ \bar{\nu_{e}} \end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix} \mu^+ \\ \bar{\nu_{\mu}} \end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix} \tau^+ \\ \bar{\nu_{\tau}} \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The first row of anti-leptons becomes +1 charge, and anti-neutrinos are right-handed.

Charged leptons interact via the electromagnetic, weak, and gravitational interactions, whereas neutrinos interact only via the weak and gravitational interactions, making them weakly interacting and difficult to detect. 
The theory predicts that the neutrinos are massless. However, neutrino oscillations are the most direct evidence that neutrinos have nonzero masses, although these masses are very small. We have yet to determine the individual neutrinos’ masses, only their upper limits. 
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{i=1}^{3} m_{\nu_i} \leq 1 \, \text{eV}
\end{equation} 
Neutrino oscillation experiments only determine the squared mass differences among the three neutrino mass eigenstates, which yield~\cite {PDG2024}

\begin{equation}
\label{mass_split}
\begin{split}
    \Delta m_{21}^2 &=m_2^2 - m_1^2 = (7.60 \pm 0.17) \times 10^{-5} \, \text{eV}^2 \\
    |\Delta m_{32}^2| &=|m_3^2 - m_2^2| = (2.52 \pm 0.04) \times 10^{-3} \, \text{eV}^2 
\end{split}
\end{equation}

Based on this information, we don't know the exact hierarchy of neutrino masses; there are two possible hierarchies: normal ($m_3 > m_2$) and inverted ($m_2 > m_3$), as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:neutrino_mass_hierarchy}.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/neutrino_mass_hierarchy.png}
\caption{The two possible neutrino mass hierarchies, normal where $m_3 > m_2$ and inverted where $m_2 > m_3$~\protect\cite{Thomson_MPP}.}
\label{fig:neutrino_mass_hierarchy}
\end{figure}


The Bosons are four Spin-1 gauge Bosons plus one Spin-0 Higgs Boson. The four spin-1 gauge Bosons are the mediators of the four fundamental forces of nature. The massless photon is the mediator of the electromagnetic force. The $W^\pm$ and Z Bosons are the mediators of the weak force. The eight color-carrying gluons are the mediators of the strong force between the quarks. There is no gauge Boson in the Standard Model for the gravitational force. In addition to gauge bosons, the Standard Model contains the Higgs boson, a scalar (spin-0) particle associated with the Higgs field, whose nonzero vacuum expectation value gives rise to the masses of elementary fermions and weak gauge bosons through spontaneous electroweak symmetry breaking.

\subsection{Role of the Higgs boson}
The electroweak theory unifies the electromagnetic and weak interactions; it predicts that the gauge bosons, fermions, and neutrinos are massless, whereas the experimental results suggest otherwise~\cite{Thomson_MPP}. In 1964, Brout, Englert, and Higgs proposed the Higgs mechanism that explains the origin of mass for those particles while preserving gauge invariance. The Higgs mechanism introduces a SU(2) doublet of a spin-0 complex field, namely the Higgs field, transforming under the electroweak gauge group \(SU(2)_L \times U(1)_Y\), 
\begin{equation}
\Phi = \begin{pmatrix} \phi^+ \\ \phi^0 \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where the $\phi^0$ and $\phi^+$ are the neutral and charged components of the Higgs field accordingly.


The Lagrangian for the Higgs field is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = (\partial_\mu\Phi)^\dagger(\partial^\mu\Phi) - V(\Phi),
\end{equation}
where the Higgs potential is 
\begin{equation}
V(\Phi) = \mu^2 \Phi^\dagger \Phi + \lambda (\Phi^\dagger \Phi)^2.
\end{equation}
For \(\mu^2 < 0\) and \(\lambda > 0\), the potential has infinite number of minima at $|\Phi| = \frac{v}{\sqrt{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{-\mu^2}{2\lambda}}.$ To ensure the photon remains massless, the vacuum state must be electrically neutral so that the electromagnetic symmetry is preserved. Therefore, the vacuum expectation value (VEV) of the Higgs field is
\begin{equation}
\langle \Phi \rangle =
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
\begin{pmatrix}
0 \\
v
\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where \(v = \sqrt{-\mu^2 / \lambda} \approx 246~\text{GeV}\) is determined experimentally. The $SU(2)_L \times U(1)_Y$ electroweak gauge symmetry is spontaneously broken down to the $U(1)_{EM}$ symmetry. One can choose the unitary gauge and write the Higgs doublet as 
\begin{equation}
\langle \Phi \rangle =
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
\begin{pmatrix}
0 \\
v + h(x)
\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
which the h(x) corresponds to the fluctuation of the vacuum state. Three of the four degrees of freedom defer to the longitudinal components of $W^\pm$ and Z bosons, while the remaining degree of freedom h(x) is the physical Higgs boson. Their masses are
\begin{equation}
m_{W^\pm} = \frac{1}{2} g v, \qquad
m_Z = \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{g^2 + g'^2}\, v, \qquad 
m_H = \sqrt{2 \lambda}\, v,
\end{equation}
with experimentally measured values of $m_{W^\pm} = 80.3625 \pm 0.0077~\mathrm{GeV}$, $m_Z = 91.1979 \pm 0.0020~\mathrm{GeV}$, and $m_H = 125.20 \pm 0.11~\mathrm{GeV}$ as reported by the Particle Data Group~\cite{PDG2024}. 

Moreover, fermions gain their mass through Yukawa interactions in the Higgs mechanism. The Lagrangian is written as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Yukawa}} = - y_f \bar{\psi}_L \Phi \psi_R + \mathrm{h.c.},
\end{equation}
where ${\psi}_L$ and $\psi_R$ are the left-handed and right-handed fermion fields, respectively; “h.c.” denotes the Hermitian conjugate, leading to fermion mass
\begin{equation}
m_f = \frac{y_f v}{\sqrt{2}},
\end{equation}
where \(y_f\) denotes the Yukawa coupling of fermion \(f\). From the equation, fermion masses are directly related to the Yukawa coupling strength with the Higgs field. For instance, the top quark has the largest mass in the Standard Model with $m_t\approx 172.5~\mathrm{GeV}$, and its Yukawa coupling, $y_t\approx 1$. In comparison, an electron has a mass of $m_e \approx 0.5~\mathrm{MeV}$ and Yukawa couplings of $y_e \approx 2.9 \times 10^{-6}$.

At the LHC, there are four primary ways to produce Higgs bosons: gluon-gluon fusion ($gg\to H$), mediated by a top-quark loop, which accounts for $\sim 88\%$ of the total Higgs production cross section, Vector Boson Fusion (VBF) at $\sim 7\%$, Higgsstrahlung (VH) at $\sim 4\%$, and Top Associated Production ($t\bar{t}H$) at $\sim 1\%$~\cite{PDG2024}. The corresponding Feynman diagrams are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:higgs_production}. Since gluons carry approximately 48$\%$ of protons’ total momentum, gluon interaction dominates over quarks when protons collide at nearly the speed of light. 
\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/higgs_production_feynman_diagram.png}
\caption{Feynman diagrams contributing to the Higgs production in (a) Gluon-Gluon fusion, (b) Vector Boson Fusion, (c) Higgsstrahlung, and (d) Top Associated Production~\protect\cite{ParticleDataGroup:2024cfk}.}
\label{fig:higgs_production}
\end{figure}

After being produced, the Higgs boson decays into several Standard Model final states. The dominant decay channel is $H\rightarrow b\bar{b}$ at $53\pm8\%$ of total decay yield, while other decay modes are $H\rightarrow WW^{*}$, $H\rightarrow ZZ^{*}$, $H\rightarrow \tau^+\tau^-$, and $H\rightarrow \gamma\gamma$ mediated by a W-boson or top-quark loop~\cite{PDG2024}. The Feynman diagrams for the diphoton decay channel are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:higgs_diphoton_decay}.

\end{figure}


Unlike the gauge bosons, the Higgs boson is not a force mediator but plays the most important role of setting the mass scale of the Standard Model. Due to its unique properties, the Higgs boson provides a sensitive gateway to potential beyond-the-Standard-Model physics.

\section{Beyond the Standard Model}
\label{sec:bsm}

The Standard Model provides a successful description of the known elementary particles and their interactions. Various experimental measurements confirm the model's predictions. However, the model has limitations that cannot explain some experimental observations, prompting researchers to search for Beyond the Standard Model (BSM) physics~\cite{PDG2024}~\cite{Thomson_MPP}.

As briefly mentioned in Subsection~\ref{subsec:brief}, electromagnetic, weak, and strong interactions, with their corresponding mediator particles, are incorporated into the quantum field theoretical framework, whereas gravity is currently understood only through classical mechanics and general relativity. The theory posits a force carrier for gravity, gravition (a massless, neutral-charged, spin-2 boson), which has not been experimentally observed. Mathematically, the theory of quantum gravity treats the force carrier as a point particle, leading to an infinite, non-renormalizable result when calculating the graviton-graviton interaction. 

The Standard Model also fails to account for the existence of dark matter, which makes up around 26.8\% of the whole universe~\cite{Planck2018}. Astrophysical and cosmological observations, including galaxy rotation curves~\cite{Rubin1980}, gravitational lensing~\cite{BulletCluster}, and measurements of the cosmic microwave background~\cite{Planck2018}, provide compelling evidence for a non-luminous form of matter that constitutes a significant fraction of the universe's energy density. However, none of the Standard Model particles satisfy the properties of dark matter. For instance, in the later Section~\ref{sec:darkphoton}, we propose the potential existence of a dark photon where the Standard Model Higgs boson can decay to a SM photon and a massless dark photon via a hypothetical mediator field. 

There is also the flavor problem that remains unexplained, concerning the three generations, the mass hierarchy, and the mixing angles. The Standard Model organized the particles into three generations, yet there is no fundamental justification for this. Secondly, the masses of the particles are determined by the Yukawa coupling constants to the Higgs field, which are experimentally determined. The mass of the electron is approximately \qty{0.511}{\mega\electronvolt/c^2}, whereas the top quark mass is about  \qty[separate-uncertainty = true]{172.56 \pm 0.32}{\giga\electronvolt/c^2}~\cite{PDG2024}. Such a drastic mass difference means there is no symmetry or principle to predict their coupling strength. Moreover, particles can transit from one flavor to another. Quark mixing is governed by the Cabibbo-Kobayashi-Maskawa (CKM) matrix, which connects their weak and mass eigenstates. Within this framework, the mixing angles are relatively small, indicating the quarks’ tendency to stay within their generations. Nevertheless, neutrino mixing is described by the Pontecorvo–Maki–Nakagawa–Sakata (PMNS) matrix, in which the mixing angles are relatively large. As a result, the neutrinos are in flavor states that are a superposition of the mass states $(\nu_1, \nu_2, \nu_3)$. The difference in mass causes a discrepancy in travel speed and phases, forming a new combination that resembles a muon neutrino rather than an electron neutrino, for example. This is the neutrino oscillation. 

Another unresolved issue is the neutrino masses. The Standard Model predicts that the neutrinos are exactly massless due to the absence of right-handed neutrino fields and Yukawa couplings. Nonetheless, experimental observations of neutrino oscillations demonstrate nonzero masses and flavor-state interconversions~\cite{SNO2002}~\cite{SuperK1998}. Explaining these masses requires introducing new particles, new interactions, or higher-dimensional operators beyond the Standard Model.

In addition, the Standard Model does not explain the observed matter--antimatter asymmetry of the universe~\cite{Sakharov1967}. Although it contains sources of CP violation in the quark sector, these effects are insufficient to account for the baryon asymmetry generated in the early universe. Additional sources of CP violation or departures from thermal equilibrium are therefore required.

All in all, these open questions encourage searches for physics beyond the Standard Model both theoretically and experimentally.

\section{Large Hadron Collider (LHC) and A Toroidal LHC Apparatus (ATLAS)}
\subsection{Introduction}

The Large Hadron Collider, or LHC for short, is a huge circular tunnel that stretches for 26.7 kilometers underground near the Swiss-French border. Inside this tunnel, protons are accelerated to nearly the speed of light and then made to collide with each other every 25 ns (40 MHz) at four interaction points: ATLAS (Interaction Point 1), CMS, LHCb, and ALICE. For this thesis, I am focusing on research in ATLAS. 

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:cern}, the process starts with a bottle of hydrogen gas, which is used to create H$^-$ ions with an electric field. Linac 4, commissioned to replace the older Linac 2, is a linear accelerator to bring the H$^-$ ions to an energy of  $160 \, \text{MeV}$. Then a carbon foil strips both electrons from the H$^-$ ions, leaving bare protons ($p^+$). 

The protons are then injected into the Proton Synchrontron Booster (PSB), Proton Synchrontron (PS), and Super Proton Synchrontron (SPS), increasing protons' energy to  $2.0 \, \text{GeV}$, $26 \, \text{GeV}$, $450 \, \text{GeV}$ respectively. Once the protons are accelerated to the required speed, they are sent to the LHC via two transfer lines (TI 2 and TI 8) and collide at the four interaction points.

A Toroidal LHC ApparatuS (ATLAS) is a general-purpose particle physics experiment at Interaction Point 1 (IP1) of the LHC~\cite{ATLASdetector}. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:atlas_detector}, the cut view of ATLAS shows that the detectors cover almost all angular directions for proton collisions at the interaction point. Its purpose is to capture all particle information produced by the collision in each layer of the detector, tailored to each particle's properties. Then, with the collected positions and deposited energy and momentum from all detectors, algorithms can identify the particle and reconstruct its track and collision vertex. For the coordinate system, the z-axis aligns with the beam axis, the x-axis points towards the center of the LHC ring, and the y-axis points upwards. Cylindrical coordinates $(r,\phi)$ are used in the transverse plane, $\phi$ being the azimuthal angle around the $z$-axis. Pseudorapidity is defined in terms of the polar angle $\theta$ as $\eta = -\ln \tan(\theta/2)$, which is Lorentz-invariant under boosts along the beamline.

\subsubsection{Luminosity}

The physics reach of the ATLAS experiment is limited by the LHC-delivered luminosity. The LHC delivered luminosity, which sets the rate of proton–proton collisions, translates into the number of expected events for a given process and its production cross section. The instantaneous luminosity, $\mathcal{L}$, can be determined from the number of observed events, N, and the corresponding production cross section, $\sigma$, by the following equation:
\begin{equation}
    N = \sigma \int \mathcal{L}(t) \, \mathrm{d}t.
\end{equation}

The data collected by ATLAS is characterized by the integrated luminosity, L, expressed in inverse femtobarns ($fb^{-1}$), which gives the total amount of data used for a particular analysis. Fig.~\ref{fig:lumi3} shows the integrated luminosity in Run 3 recorded by ATLAS at 322 $fb^{-1}$, and Fig.~\ref{fig:lumi123} illustrates the combined Run 1, 2, 3 integrated luminosity recorded by ATLAS at 505 $fb^{-1}$.

Higher luminosity means more collision events, enabling more precise measurements of particle mass and coupling strength. However, the increased number of particles hitting the detectors means that signals are buried in a large background of other particles, making track reconstruction more difficult.

\subsubsection{Pile-up}

Pile-up refers to multiple proton-proton interactions occurring within a single bunch crossing. It is commonly quantified by the parameter $\mu$, which represents the average number of inelastic interactions per bunch crossing. Higher luminosity operation of the LHC directly leads to increased pile-up.

Pile-up interactions contribute additional charged particles and energy deposits that are unrelated to the primary hard-scatter process and to the secondary vertex from $b$-quark decays, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:pileup}. These additional interactions can affect track reconstruction, vertex identification, jet energy measurements, and missing transverse momentum reconstruction. As a result, pile-up represents a major experimental challenge, particularly in later LHC runs where luminosity is substantially higher.

To mitigate the effects of pile-up, ATLAS uses a combination of detector design and specialized techniques to reconstruct what happened. This includes precise vertex reconstruction and pile-up-aware object definitions. It's also crucial to accurately model pile-up in Monte Carlo simulations to match real data through reweighting. By doing this, they can better understand and account for the impact of pile-up.

\subsection{Timeline of LHC and ATLAS Data-Taking Runs}

A brief overview of the LHC run timeline and the ATLAS data-taking is given below. Each operational period of the experiment is called a run and is characterized by higher center-of-mass collision energy, higher luminosity, and improved detector and software performance.

---

\subsubsection{Run 1 (2010--2012)}

The first physics run of the LHC was Run 1. The LHC delivered proton-proton collisions at a center-of-mass energy of $\sqrt{s}=7~\mathrm{TeV}$ in 2010 and 2011, and $8~\mathrm{TeV}$ in 2012 with an integrated luminosity of $\sim 25~fb^{-1}$ recorded for physics. The ATLAS detector was commissioned for physics, and its performance for tracking, calorimetry, muon detection, and triggers was established.
The Higgs boson was discovered in 2012 by the ATLAS and CMS experiments at the LHC with a statistical significance of 5$\sigma$. Run 1 has proven the basic design of the ATLAS detector and has identified limitations regarding radiation-hard components, trigger rate, and tracking performance that can be addressed in subsequent upgrades. 

---

\subsubsection{Run 2 (2015--2018)}
During Run 2, the LHC was operating at a center-of-mass energy of $\sqrt{s}=13~\mathrm{TeV}$ with an integrated luminosity of $\sim 140~fb^{-1}$ recorded for physics, nearly doubling the energy available in Run~1. This allowed many physics processes to be studied in greater detail, including the Higgs boson, top quarks, electroweak processes, and searches for physics beyond the Standard Model. 

A major upgrade of the ATLAS experiment for Run 2 was the installation of the Insertable B-Layer (IBL) around the beam pipe at a distance of about 50-60 mm from the interaction point. The IBL is one of the highest-pixel-density tracking detectors ever built and, together with improved vertexing, significantly improved the detector's performance. For the same reason as the increased luminosity in subsequent years, improvements to the trigger and track reconstruction software were significant. 

---

\subsubsection{Run 3 (2022--2026)}

During Run 3, the LHC reached the center-of-mass energy of $\sqrt{s} = 13.6\,\text{ TeV}$ with an integrated luminosity of more than $300~fb^{-1}$ recorded for physics. Even though the collision energy increases only moderately, the higher luminosity (more than Run 1 and 2 combined) and more pile-up pose greater challenges.

The upgraded trigger and data acquisition system was deployed for full testing in Run 3, enabling the full exploitation of the ATLAS tracking system. Sophisticated real-time reconstruction and tracking techniques in a high-pile-up environment were validated and employed, in preparation for Run4~\cite{ATLASRun3Overview}.

---

\subsubsection{Long Shutdown 3 and Run 4 (HL-LHC Era)}

During the third long shutdown of the LHC (LS3), the collider will be upgraded to the High-Luminosity LHC (HL-LHC), with the goal of delivering an integrated luminosity of approximately $3$--$4~\mathrm{ab}^{-1}$ \cite{HLLHCDesign}, which is 10x that of Run 3. ATLAS will undergo major Phase-II upgrades, including the complete replacement of the Inner Detector with the all-silicon Inner Tracker (ITk)~\cite{ATLASITkTDR}.

At HL-LHC, new aspects of tracking, triggers, and data processing have to be incorporated into the experimental analysis. In part, this will be done by more parallel computing. Also, hardware acceleration (FPGAs and GPUs) will play a role. Measurements of the Higgs coupling will have lower uncertainty. Also, rare processes in the Standard Model will be studied in detail. Eventually, new physics will also be searched with increased sensitivity.

\subsection{The ATLAS Detector}

The Inner Detector (ID) is the innermost tracking system in ATLAS and reconstructs the trajectories of charged particles produced in proton--proton collisions, which consists of pixel and strip detectors and Transition Radiation Tracker (TRT, which will be removed before Run 4). Operating in a 2-tesla solenoidal magnetic field enables precise measurements of charged-particle momenta via curvature. The trajectories and transverse momentum allow for accurate measurement of track parameters, particle identification, and vertex positions. The inner tracker is also used to calculate neutral-particle counts and missing transverse energy when combined with data from other detectors. 

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/ATLAS Detector Schematic black particles.png}
    \caption{ATLAS detector slice with labeled subdetectors and particle visualizations \protect\cite{Mehlhase:2770815}}
    \label{fig:detector}
\end{figure}


The calorimeter system surrounds the Inner Detector and is designed to measure particle energies through interactions with dense absorber materials. It is divided into electromagnetic and hadronic calorimeters.
Electrons and photons disperse their energy by creating electromagnetic showers in electromagnetic calorimeters. Electrons and photons have enough energy to undergo bremsstrahlung (emit an additional photon) and pair production (create an electron-positron pair), respectively, triggering cascades until they lose enough energy to be absorbed, and the remaining energy is detected by the detectors. While gluons and quarks, due to color confinement, tend to form bound states that produce collimated particle jets, they shower hadronically in the electromagnetic calorimeters and complete the process in the hadronization calorimeters. 

The calorimeter system provides nearly full angular coverage and is essential for jet reconstruction, electron and photon identification, and the determination of missing transverse momentum. In addition to energy measurements, calorimeter information is used for particle identification and plays a key role in the trigger system.

The Muon Spectrometer forms the outermost detector system of ATLAS and is dedicated to identifying and measuring muon momentum. Muons have minimal energy loss as they pass through all the detectors, so they are detected by ionization in the Muon Chamber, and their overall momentum and energy are calculated from data in the Inner Detector and Muon Chamber.


Missing transverse energy ($E_T^miss$) is calculated from the transverse 
momentum symmetry of the initial proton collision. Since the beamline is in the z direction, any imbalance in the transverse momentum of the detected particle indicates missing transverse energy in the other direction. These are mainly contributed by SM neutrinos and BDM particles, which weakly interact with detectors. As a result, all the detector results must be included in deriving the missing transverse energy.

\subsection{Trigger and Data Acquisition System}

The high collision rate delivered by the LHC far exceeds the rate at which events can be recorded for offline analysis. To address this challenge, ATLAS employs a trigger and data acquisition system that selects potentially interesting events in real time while rejecting most collisions.

The trigger system reduces the event rate through a multi-level selection process based on information from the detector subsystems. Trigger decisions are designed to maintain high efficiency in the physics processes of interest while operating within strict latency and bandwidth constraints. The data acquisition system then records the selected events for further processing and offline reconstruction. Together, the trigger and data acquisition systems enable ATLAS to efficiently exploit the LHC's physics potential.

\section{ATLAS Run 4 Upgrade}
\label{sec:run4}

In Run 4, the Large Hadron Collider will enter the High-Luminosity LHC (HL-LHC) phase, which is designed to significantly extend the ATLAS experiment's physics reach. The HL-LHC aims to deliver an integrated luminosity of approximately $3$--$4~\mathrm{ab}^{-1}$ over its operational lifetime, corresponding to more than an order-of-magnitude increase relative to the datasets collected in earlier runs. This increase will enable precise measurements of rare Standard Model processes and enhance sensitivity to physics beyond the Standard Model.

The higher luminosity of the HL-LHC will result in substantially more challenging experimental conditions. The instantaneous luminosity increases to an average pile-up level of up to $\langle\mu\rangle \sim 200$ interactions per bunch crossing. Such conditions will significantly increase detector occupancies, radiation exposure, and the complexity of event reconstruction. Maintaining the performance of tracking, vertexing, and triggering under these conditions requires major upgrades to the ATLAS detector systems.

A key component of the Phase-II upgrade program is the complete replacement of the current Inner Detector with a new all-silicon Inner Tracker (ITk). In Run 3, the primary tracker system of the ATLAS detector is the Inner Detector (ID), which consists of Pixel detectors, SemiConductor Trackers (SCT or strip detectors), and Transition Radiation Trackers (TRT)~\cite{GarciaAlonso:2024itk}. The Pixel and Strip detectors cover a pseudorapidity ($\eta$) range of $|\eta| < 2.5$ (TRT covers $|\eta| < 2.0$) with radiation tolerance designed for an integrated luminosity of $300\text{--}400\,\text{fb}^{-1}$.

For the High-Luminosity LHC (HL-LHC) upgrade, the ID will be replaced by the ATLAS Inner Tracker (ITK), the primary tracker system, which eliminates the TRT and replaces ID Pixel and Strip with ITK pixel (Inner) and ITK strip (outer) detectors. The ITK pixel will cover the pseudorapidity ($\eta$) range of $|\eta| < 4.0$, where the ITK strip covers $|\eta| < 2.7$. The designed radiation tolerance is upgraded to withstand an integrated luminosity of $4000\,\text{fb}^{-1}$. Moreover, the number of modules will be increased from 6000 to 28000; the number of channels will be increased from $100 \times 10^6$ to more than $5 \times 10^9$ in ITK. 

In parallel, the ATLAS trigger and data acquisition system will be upgraded to cope with the increased event rates and data volumes expected during HL-LHC operation, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:Trigger}. The upgraded trigger system adds L0-level processing for Calorimeters and the Muon System, as well as an Event Filter for Dataflow processing. The readout data rate increases from 100 kHz to 1 MHz, and the output data rate from 3 kHz to 10 kHz. 

The ATLAS Phase-II upgrade program is therefore essential for sustaining detector performance and physics sensitivity throughout the HL-LHC era. The design choices and technological developments implemented during this upgrade phase directly address the challenges posed by high luminosity and pile-up~\cite{HLLHCTDR}~\cite{ATLASPhaseIITDR}.

\chapter{ATLAS Event Filter (EF) Tracking: Strip Clustering}
\section{Introduction}

This report presents performance studies for the Event Filter (EF) Tracking project of the ATLAS experiment, part of its Phase-II upgrade program for the High-Luminosity LHC (HL-LHC). The HL-LHC pile-up conditions will produce an average of 200 inelastic proton--proton collisions per bunch crossing, and a new full-silicon Inner Tracker (ITk)~\cite{itk_strip_tdr, itk_pixel_tdr}, designed to allow efficient tracking in such an environment, will be installed during the Phase-II upgrade. The high-pile-up conditions pose a significant challenge to the ATLAS TDAQ system, whose architecture and physics motivations are described in the Phase-II TDAQ TDR~\cite{tdaq_tdr} and the Phase-II TDAQ TDR Amendment~\cite{tdaq_tdr_amend}. All the EF algorithms, including the EF Tracking ones, will run on a flexible, heterogeneous commercial system comprising CPU cores, possibly assisted by commodity accelerators such as GPUs or FPGAs. The rate at which events can be reconstructed for a given amount of computational hardware is a critical design factor for the EF system, with tracking historically the most computationally intensive reconstruction performed at the EF stage. The TDAQ design foresees that events are first processed by a hardware trigger (Level-0), which bases its decision on calorimeter and muon detector data. Accepted events are passed to the EF, which collects data from all detector readout systems at a maximum rate of 1~MHz. Following EF processing, the event acceptance rate is reduced to 10~kHz. Track reconstruction will play a critical role in the subsequent trigger decisions, helping to keep the EF output rate at a manageable level. It can be used to refine the selection of objects identified at Level-0, improve the precision of their kinematic measurements, and suppress multi-object coincidences, for instance by requiring that associated tracks originate from the same primary vertex. Track reconstruction is, however, more challenging due to the high processing rate and the large hit density in the tracking detectors, which makes pattern recognition more computationally demanding. At the same time, the optimized layout of the ITk is expected to reduce the fake-track rate to a negligible level~\cite{itk_strip_tdr, itk_pixel_tdr}.

To choose an EF Tracking design adapted to those challenges, the ATLAS Phase-II EF Tracking community embarked on a series of demonstrator cycles to study so-called tracking pipelines. In parallel, performance requirements for the EF tracking algorithms as well as the EF farm were produced, motivated by PPES studies aimed at the HL-LHC physics goals of ATLAS~\cite{trigger_studies_hl_lhc}. Following this, the design selection criteria and process were established. Both the requirements and criteria were reviewed and approved by a committee composed of various experts from within and outside of the ATLAS TDAQ community.

The studies presented in this report explore a possible EF Tracking design that uses GPU accelerators alongside conventional CPU processing. Tracking algorithms are put together to form a tracking pipeline. The pipeline referred to as C-000 is the default ITk track reconstruction which uses the legacy implementation of the tracking algorithms in ATHENA and serves as a reference for the comparisons of the various metrics. Several pipelines are described, with varying amounts of processing performed on GPU as indicated by the first digit: from G-0XX (minimal offloading) to G-2XX (full offloading). 

\subsection{Overview of GPU-based pipelines}
\label{sec:gpu_pipelines_overview}

The framework known as A Common Tracking Software (ACTS)~\cite{acts_2017} represents a robust, high-performance solution for track reconstruction in Particle Physics, usable across multiple experiments. Designed with modularity, detector independence, and adaptability to contemporary computing architectures in mind, ACTS has been successfully integrated into the ATHENA software framework of the ATLAS experiment. It is currently slated to replace the existing offline tracking software for LHC Run 4 and subsequent operations. Furthermore, ACTS has been proposed as a key component for the Event Filter (EF) online track reconstruction, particularly within the C-100 and C-230 pipelines~\cite{c100_pipeline_report}.

TRACCC~\cite{traccc_github} is a dedicated GPU-oriented research and development extension of ACTS, independently developed to address the stringent performance requirements of online track reconstruction in dense, high-rate detector environments. While TRACCC shares foundational algorithms, supporting libraries, and detector descriptions with ACTS, it is a separate piece of software designed to exploit the massive parallelism inherent in GPU hardware and to achieve portability across varying GPU hardware and software frameworks. This report focuses on the deployment of TRACCC within multiple types of GPU-based processing pipelines.

Within the pipelines denoted G-2X0, the entire track reconstruction sequence---including clustering, space point formation, seeding, track finding, and track fitting---is executed on the GPU. The CPU is used solely for transferring reconstructed tracks from GPU memory to host memory and for converting between TRACCC and ATHENA EDM. Other pipelines transition from GPU to CPU at different stages of the track reconstruction chain, executing the remaining reconstruction steps on the CPU. This GPU-CPU hybrid architecture leverages the strengths of both processing units to optimize overall performance, hypothesising that the most parallelizable and least branching stages---such as clustering and seeding---benefit most from GPU acceleration. The remaining steps use the ACTS algorithms in a workflow equivalent to that of the C-100/C-230 pipelines. The hybrid pipelines differ in which stages of track reconstruction are offloaded to the GPU: G-0X0 pipelines offload only the clustering step, G-1X0 pipelines offload both clustering and track seeding, G-2X5 offload clustering, seeding and track finding. Since my study focuses on a GPU-based strip clustering algorithm, this paper will focus on performance and efficiency in the G-080 pipeline.

\subsubsection{Motivation for GPU Implementation}

Silicon strip clustering exhibits intrinsic parallelism. Clusters are formed independently within each detector module, and individual strip hits can be processed concurrently when searching for neighboring channels above threshold. This structure makes the algorithm well-suited for GPU execution, where thousands of lightweight threads can operate simultaneously.

However, achieving efficient GPU performance requires more than simply parallelizing loops. Memory layout, coalesced access patterns, thread mapping strategies, and synchronization between neighboring strips must be carefully designed to avoid performance bottlenecks. Additionally, the algorithm must reproduce the clustering behavior of the reference Athena implementation to ensure consistency in downstream tracking performance.

The motivation for implementing strip clusterization on GPUs within \texttt{traccc} is therefore twofold:

\begin{itemize}
    \item To reduce the computational latency of clustering in high-occupancy environments.
    \item To establish a validated GPU-based tracking workflow compatible with ATLAS software standards.
\end{itemize}

Demonstrating physics equivalence while achieving computational gains is essential for future integration into trigger or offline reconstruction chains.

\subsection{Strip clustering algorithm}
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/eftracking/itk_geometry.png}
    \caption{The layout of the ATLAS ITK, where the red represents the pixel detectors, and the blue represents the strip detectors (horizontal blue lines are barrel, and vertical are annulus)~\protect\cite{ATL-PHYS-PUB-2021-024}.}
    \label{fig:itk}
\end{figure}

The strip clusterization is performed by means of a Connected Component Analysis (CCA) algorithm. The readout of the ATLAS strip detectors is binary, i.e. hit or no-hit. A charged particle crossing a layer of a silicon sensor generates electron-hole pairs which drift to the readout strips and are collected by the adjacent readout strips. As a result, the charge generated by a charged particle is distributed among a number of adjacent readout strips. Therefore, a charged particle crossing a layer of a silicon detector is detected as a cluster of adjacent fired readout strips. A set of adjacent fired readout strips is called a cluster within this algorithm. The uncorrected cluster position, or centroid ($x_{\text{cluster}}$) of a cluster, is calculated using the following equation: \begin{equation} x_{\text{cluster}} = \frac{x_{\text{first}} + x_{\text{last}}}{2} \end{equation} where $x_{\text{first}}$ and $x_{\text{last}}$ are the local spatial coordinates of the first and last strips in the cluster. 

The strip detectors in ATLAS are mounted inside a $2\,\text{T}$ solenoid, resulting in the electron-hole pairs generated by charged particles passing through the sensors being subject to the Lorentz force, $\vec{F} = q\vec{E} \times \vec{B}$. This causes the charge carriers to drift at an angle $\theta_L$, known as the Lorentz angle, relative to the electric field lines. As a result of this angled drift, the collected charge distribution is systematically shifted along the transverse plane. To recover the particle's true intersection point with the sensor, a correction must be applied to the cluster centroid. The magnitude of this Lorentz shift ($\Delta x_L$) evaluated at the mid-plane of the sensor is given by: \begin{equation} \Delta x_L = \frac{d}{2} \tan(\theta_L) \end{equation} where $d$ is the active thickness of the silicon sensor. The corrected local $x$-coordinate is thus: \begin{equation} x_{\text{corrected}} = x_{\text{cluster}} - \Delta x_L \end{equation} The sign of $\Delta x_L$ is dependent on the detector module’s orientation with respect to the magnetic field.

Once the Lorentz shift corrects for the $E \times B$ drift of charge carriers in the magnetic field, the hit coordinates are evaluated in the \textbf{Beam Frame (BF)}. For the ITk strip endcaps, which utilize annulus-shaped modules, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:annulus}, these coordinates must subsequently be shifted into the \textbf{Strip Frame (SF)} due to its shape.

The transformation shifts the origin of the coordinate system from the beamline to the specific focus point of the annulus strips ($f$). In Cartesian coordinates, this involves a translation to the module origin, a rotation by the stereo angle $\phi_s$, and a final translation to the strip focus. 

Mathematically, this transformation is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
\begin{pmatrix} x_{SF} \\ y_{SF} \end{pmatrix} = 
\begin{bmatrix} 
\cos(-\phi_s) & -\sin(-\phi_s) \\ 
\sin(-\phi_s) & \cos(-\phi_s) 
\end{bmatrix} 
\begin{pmatrix} x_{BF} - R \\ y_{BF} \end{pmatrix} + 
\begin{pmatrix} R \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
where $R$ is the radial distance to the module origin.

\subsection{Integration with the \texttt{Traccc} framework}
The input data include the strip ID (stripID) and the number of fired strips (nFiredStrip). For instance, if stripID is zero and nFiredStrip is 3, then the stripID is 0, 1, 2 as input to the strip clustering algorithm. For strip modules, a digitization configuration is required to log the cell information to their corresponding modules. The configuration includes the volume, layer, sensitive information, module rows and columns, width, and length. Since strip modules are one-dimensional, the barrel has only one module column, and the module rows are the number of cells in that module. For annulus modules, due to their parabolic shape, the module rows and width are recorded as the number of cells and theta relative to the center in the beam frame, where the module columns and length are one and the radius from the center. After clustering, the Lorentz shift is applied to the annulus modules, and the local positions are then transformed from the beam frame to the strip frame.

\chapter{ATLAS: Signal Optimization of A Search for Higgs Decays to Dark Photons using ATLAS Run 3 Data}
\label{sec:darkphoton}
\section{Title}
Search for dark photons from Higgs boson decays in the gluon-gluon fusion channel in proton-proton collisions at $\sqrt{s}=13.6$ TeV with the ATLAS detector
\section{Disclaimer}
This chapter will be published as a co-authored paper; the credit belongs to the ATLAS Collaboration. See reference~\cite {ATLAS-HMBS-2024-50-002} for a link to the latest draft to publication.
\section{Abstract}
This paper presents a search for semi-visible Higgs boson decays in final states with a photon ($\gamma$) and missing transverse momentum $p_T^{miss}$.
The analysis is optimised for the gluon-gluon fusion production channel and exploits a novel trigger deployed in 2023, with reduced thresholds down to 50 GeV on the photon transverse momentum ($p_T^{\gamma}$) and 70 GeV on the $p_T^{miss}$, in combination with a selection on the transverse mass of the $p_T^{miss}$-$\gamma$ system ($m_T$) above 80 GeV. The results are interpreted in terms of Higgs boson decays into a $\gamma$ and a dark photon $\gamma_d$ ($H \to \gamma \gamma_d$).
The analysis is performed using 135 fb$^{-1}$ of Run 3 data collected by ATLAS in 2023 and 2024. A boosted decision tree is used to identify events with misreconstructed primary vertices, which can affect the $p_T^{miss}$ calculation. Data-driven methods are employed to estimate the backgrounds from jets and electrons misidentified as photons, while the backgrounds with genuine photons are taken from Monte Carlo simulations, normalised to data in control regions with muons. 
A binned maximum-likelihood fit to the $m_T$ distribution is performed to evaluate the compatibility of the observed data with the Standard Model expectation. No significant excess is observed over the Standard Model prediction, and an observed (expected) upper limit on the branching ratio of $H \to \gamma \gamma_d$ of 1.4\% (1.2\%) is obtained at 95\% confidence level. Combining this result with previous Run 2 searches improves the observed (expected) upper limit to 0.9\% (0.9\%).

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Motivation for Hidden Sectors}

While Section~\ref{sec:bsm} gives a brief overview of the astrophysical evidence for dark matter, the Standard Model offers no viable particle candidate. This motivates the hypothesis of a Dark Section (DS), in which the dark photon ($\gamma_d$) is the gauge boson of an additional \( U(1)_D \) gauge group. Strong motivations for the search for this particle arise from its potential contribution in solving the small-scale structure formation problems \cite{Fan:2013tia} \cite{Heikinheimo:2015kra}, as well as being a possible source for the Sommerfeld enhancement of DM annihilation required to explain the PAMELA-Fermi-MS2 positron anomaly in terms of DM \cite{Arkani-Hamed:2008hhe}, or making asymmetric DM scenarios viable \cite{Zurek:2013wia}.

\subsection{Dark Photon}

The dark photon, denoted $A'_\mu$, is associated with a new $U(1)_D$ gauge symmetry. 

The decay of the Higgs boson into a photon and a dark photon, $H\to\gamma\gamma_d$ provides an interesting production channel for this BSM particle. The study focuses on a massless dark photon, which cannot directly couple to Standard Model (SM) particles \cite{Fabbrichesi_2021}.
In this case, the kinetic mixing between the $\gamma_d$ and the photon can be tuned away through a rotation of the gauge fields, and the $\gamma_d$ can interact with ordinary matter only through operators of dimension higher
than four \cite{Fabbrichesi_2021}. The Higgs boson can decay through a loop interaction involving additional (pseudo-)scalar BSM messenger fields, which couple both to the SM and the DS~\cite{Gabrielli:2014oya, Biswas:2016jsh}, as shown in 
Fig.~\ref{fig:Feynman-all-Hyyd}. Thanks to the non-decoupling properties of the Higgs boson, and depending on the parameters of the model, a branching ratio (BR) of $H\to\gamma\gamma_d$ of a few percent can be allowed ~\cite{Beauchesne2023a, Beauchesne2023b}.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{figures/feynman_ggHyyd.pdf}

    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{figures/feynman_VBFHyyd.pdf}
    \hspace{.1\textwidth}
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{figures/feynman_VHyyd.pdf}

The hatched circle in the $H\gamma\gamma_d$ vertex represents the interaction through messenger fields.}
    \label{fig:Feynman-all-Hyyd}

Searches for the $ \gamma + E_{\text{T}}^{\text{miss}} + X $ signature have been widely performed at the LHC by the ATLAS and CMS experiments, in both the vector boson fusion (VBF) production mode and associated production with a $Z$ boson (ZH)~\cite{HDBS-2019-13, EXOT-2021-17, EXOT-2018-63, CMS-EXO-19-007, EXOT-2014-06, CMS-EXO-12-047, CMS-HIG-14-025, CMS-EXO-20-005}. A combination of ATLAS results in the ZH and VBF channels currently provides the most stringent limits, excluding $\mathrm{BR}(H \to \gamma\gamma_d) > 1.3\%$ ($1.5\%$ expected) at $95\%$~CL. This result is based on an integrated luminosity of \qty{139}{\ifb} collected during LHC Run~2 at $\sqrt{s} = 13$~TeV~\cite{ATLAS:2024cju}.

Though these two production modes provide clean final states, the gluon-gluon fusion one (ggF) is an order of magnitude larger in cross-section than the VBF channel. This channel has been explored by CMS ~\cite{CMS-HIG-14-025}, but not yet by ATLAS -- though the inclusive signature with a single photon has been pursued in the context of other exotic models~\cite{EXOT-2018-63}. 
One challenge is that this kind of signature was not accessible via the available triggers due to their high photon $p_{\text{T}}$ or $E_{\text{T}}^{\text{miss}}$ thresholds. Developments in the trigger between Run-2 and Run-3 led to the implementation of a lower threshold trigger, which increases the acceptance of ggF events by approximately a factor of two, while keeping the trigger rate under control. 

The analysis presented in this paper targets the $H\to\gamma\gamma_d$ decay in the ggF production channel for the first time in ATLAS, retaining sensitivity also to the VBF, ZH and WH channels. 
The analysis is based on the LHC Run-3 proton–proton collisions data at $\sqrt{s}=13.6$~TeV recorded by the ATLAS experiment in 2023 and 2024, corresponding to an integrated luminosity of 134.6~\ifb.

\subsection{MC samples}

The MC samples were processed with p-tag p6697, which labels the specific ATLAS production campaign and the processing configuration used to generate the data.

The signal samples were showered from common Higgs Les Houches Event (LHE) samples generated using Powheg. The decay process used in the shower was implemented with Pythia~\cite{Nason:2004rx,Frixione:2007vw,Alioli:2010xd}. The signal sample paths are shown in table~\ref{tab:grl_summary}. 

The primary backgrounds for the analysis include processes with a true photon in the final states and genuine $E_{T}^{miss}$. These samples include: $Z\gamma$ and $W\gamma$, where the vector boson can decay to a lepton that is not reconstructed or to neutrinos.
Additional background processes arise from fake photons due to misidentified jets or electrons, and/or fake $E_{T}^{miss}$. 
The background from jets or electrons misidentified as photons is mainly given by $Zjets$, $Wjets$, di-jets, and $\gamma jets$ with a fragmentation photon. The $Zjets$, di-jets, and $\gamma(frag) jets$ events are also characterized by fake $E_{T}^{miss}$ due to mis-measured or not reconstructed jets. Finally, $\gamma jets$ with direct photon ($\gamma (direct)jets$) enter the analysis regions exclusively due to the presence of fake $E_{T}^{miss}$. \\
The list of MC samples used in this analysis is presented in the Appendix tables in Chapter~\ref{ch:chapter}. As detailed later, MC simulations are ultimately employed only for true photon backgrounds (including the $\gamma(direct) $ jets), while the contribution from events with fake photons is estimated using data-driven methods. The $W/Z$ MC samples are used as part of the \jfakey method and therefore listed in the following tables, while $\gamma(direct) jets$ and di-jets have been used exclusively for preliminary studies and initial optimization of the analysis selection and are not reported here.

\section{Physics object reconstruction and identification}

Physics objects used in this analysis are reconstructed following standard ATLAS procedures. Photon candidates are reconstructed from topological clusters in the electromagnetic calorimeter and calibrated using an energy calibration chain, and are required to satisfy tight identification criteria based on shower-shape variables together with isolation requirements designed to suppress hadronic backgrounds~\cite{ATLASPhotonPerf}. Muons are reconstructed by combining tracks from the inner detector and the muon spectrometer, and are selected using quality and isolation working points that ensure high efficiency while suppressing non-prompt backgrounds~\cite{ATLASMuonPerf}. Electrons are formed by matching inner-detector tracks to electromagnetic calorimeter clusters and are identified using likelihood-based discriminants exploiting shower shapes, track quality, and track–cluster matching observables~\cite{ATLASElectronPerf}. Jets are reconstructed using the anti-$k_t$ algorithm with radius parameter $R=0.4$ applied to particle-flow or topological calorimeter inputs, and are calibrated through simulation-based and in situ corrections, with additional pile-up mitigation techniques such as jet-vertex tagging applied~\cite{ATLASJetPerf}. To avoid double-counting of detector signatures, reconstructed objects are subjected to an overlap-removal procedure that resolves ambiguities among nearby photons, leptons, and jets using a fixed priority scheme~\cite{ATLASObjDef}. The missing transverse momentum, $\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{miss}}$, is computed as the negative vector sum of the transverse momenta of all reconstructed baseline objects, supplemented by a track-based soft term associated with the primary vertex to reduce pile-up sensitivity~\cite{ATLASMETPerf}. These definitions ensure a consistent and well-validated reconstruction of final-state objects across ATLAS analyses.

\subsection{Trigger}
The analysis uses a trigger implemented in 2023. 
The trigger requires a single photon at 26 GeV at L1 -- which is shared with the single-electron L1 item. The HLT is a multi-object trigger that requires a 50 GeV tight ID photon (g50), 40 GeV of cell $E_{T}^{miss}$ (xe40\_cell) and 70 GeV of ``pfopufit'' $E_{T}^{miss}$ (xe70\_pfopufit). There transverse mass computed between the photon and the $E_{T}^{miss}$ is defined using the expression: $m_T = \sqrt{2 E_{T}^{\gamma}E_{T}^{miss} (1-cos(\Delta \phi))}$. The trigger requires that: $m_T > 80$ GeV be applied (\texttt{80mTAC}).\\

This trigger is consistently applied in all the analysis regions, which are generally characterized by signatures allowing reasonable trigger acceptance: $1\mu+\gamma$, $2\mu+\gamma$, 1 non-isolated $\gamma+E_{T}^{miss}$, $1e+E_{T}^{miss}$. For the muon-CRs, it is possible to apply the analysis trigger taking advantage of the fact that muons are not included in the trigger-level $E_{T}^{miss}$ computation. The most critical final state is $1e+E_{T}^{miss}$: in this case, an OR of multiple single-electron triggers and the analysis trigger is applied.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/df_iteration_workflow.png}

\caption{Iteration workflow for optimizing the cut set using n-1 studies and significance checks.}

The iteration process, demonstrated in figure ~\ref{fig:iteration_workflow}, determines the optimized cuts for the additional selections. After applying preselection cuts, it imposes lower and upper bounds on the key variables, denoted $c_i$ for clarity, based on their ROC curves. If either an upper/lower cut does not improve significance, we will skip the cut for this variable, but it will be reconsidered and iterated in a later process. Combining all the cuts becomes our initial cuts of the variables. Then it performs n-1 cuts: for each cut $c_i$, apply all the cuts except $c_i$ and calculate the n-1 significance. Choose the cut value of $c_i$ that maximizes the significance, and repeat the process for the next $c_{i+1}$. If the n-1 significance is greater without the cut, we remove the cut. In the next iteration, it will reconsider the entire list of cut variables and cut values, including those previously dropped. We finalize the optimized cuts and significance if none of the chosen cuts change, and there is no further improvement in significance in one iteration.

\subsection{Statistical method}
\label{sec:stat_method}
The combination is done using a combined likelihood function, i.e. the product of the likelihoods for the individual channels times the product of the likelihoods for the final observables in each of the analysis categories within a single analysis.

In the above combination, the common parameter of interest (POI) for all channels to be combined is the ratio $H \rightarrow \gamma \gamma_d$ branching ratio, denoted by $\mu$.
The systematic uncertainties are modeled as nuisance parameters $\bm{\theta}$, which are treated as Gaussian variables.
They represent information from auxiliary measurements and parameterize the uncertainty of that information, i.e. the (different) uncertainty of a systematic to mimic the signal in the current combination.
In the fit across the various backgrounds, the corresponding normalization, $\bm{\kappa}$, is also treated as a free parameter without any constraints. This parameter is used to achieve the best possible agreement with the data in the corresponding CR(s) for the background shapes.

The combined likelihood can be written as~\cite{Cranmer:1456844}
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}\left(\mathrm{data}\vert\mu,\bm{\kappa},\bm{\theta}\right) = \prod_{c=1}^{N_\mathrm{cats}}\mathcal{L}_c\left(\mathrm{data}\vert\mu,\bm{\kappa},\bm{\theta}\right)\prod_{k=1}^{N_\mathrm{cons}}\mathcal{G}\left(\tilde{\theta}_k\vert\theta_k\right),
    \label{eq:combined_likelihood}
\end{equation}
where $N_\mathrm{cats}$ is the number of categories, $N_\mathrm{cons}$ is the number of constrained NPs, $\tilde{\theta}_k$ is the global observable corresponding to $\theta_k$, $c$ is the index for the event categories, $k$ is the index for the constrained NPs, and $\mathcal{G}$ represents a Gaussian distribution.

The likelihood for a given category $\mathcal{L}_c$ can be expressed as the product of Poisson distributions, one for each bin in the histogram of the observable considered for that category, as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_c\left(\mathrm{data}\vert\mu,\bm{\kappa},\bm{\theta}\right) = \prod_{i=1}^{N_c^\mathrm{bins}}\mathcal{P}\left(n^i_c\middle\vert\sum_{S} S^i_{c}(\mu,\bm{\theta}) + \sum_{B}B^i_{c}(\bm{\kappa},\bm{\theta}) \right),
    \label{eq:cat_likelihood}
\end{equation}
where $N^\mathrm{bins}_c$ is the number of bins, $i$ is the bin index, $\mathcal{P}$ denotes a Poisson distribution, $n^i_c$ is the number of (observed or Asimov) data events in each bin, $S^i_{c}$ and $B^i_{c}$ are the expected yields of a given signal sample $S$ and background sample $B$, respectively, in each bin.

The $\mathrm{CL_s}$ frequentist method~\cite{Read:2002hq} following the asymptotic formulae~\cite{Cowan:2010js} is used to derive the 95\% CL upper limit on the POI with the profile likelihood ratio test statistic $(q_\mu)$, defined as
\begin{equation}
    q_\mu = -2\ln\frac{\mathcal{L}\left(\mu,\hat{\hat{\bm{\kappa}}}(\mu), \hat{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}(\mu)\right)}{\mathcal{L}\left(\hat{\mu},{\hat{\bm{\kappa}}}, {\hat{\bm{\theta}}}\right)},
    \label{eq:qmu}
\end{equation}
where the numerator indicates the values of $\bm{\kappa}$ and $\bm{\theta}$ that maximize $\mathcal{L}$ for a given value of $\mu$, and the denominator is evaluated for the values $\hat{\mu}$, ${\hat{\bm{\kappa}}}$, ${\hat{\bm{\theta}}}$ which which jointly maximize the likelihood.

A binned maximum-likelihood fit is performed simultaneously in the SR and in the CRs, using the transverse mass $m_T$ as the discriminating variable, in order to maximize the sensitivity to the Higgs boson mass peak. All regions entering the fit are binned in $m_{T}$, with the exception of the 2$\mu$ CR, which is treated as a single inclusive bin due to limited statistical precision.

Figure~\ref{fig:mt-prefit-postfit} is the comparison of the $m_T$ distributions before and after the application of additional selection cuts. In this context, the ``pre'' distributions correspond to the baseline event selection, while the ``post'' distributions are obtained after applying the additional optimized requirements.
The $m_T$ distributions span $80$--$300$~GeV with binning (80, 110, 140, 200, 300)~GeV, where the optimization for the selection cuts was performed in the region of $m_T$ between 100 and 140 GeV. 

The likelihood function is constructed as the product of Poisson probability density functions describing the expected signal and background yields in each $m_T$ bin. The branching ratio BR($H\to\gamma\gamma_d$) is included in the fit as the Parameter of Interest (POI). The two normalization factors, $k_{W_{\gamma}}$ and $k_{Z_{\gamma}}$, are applied to the $W_\gamma$ and $Z_\gamma$ background processes respectively. 

With allowing the $k_{W_{\gamma}}$ and $k_{Z_{\gamma}}$ to flow in the fit, the fitted normalization factors are $k_{W\gamma} = 1.09^{+0.09}_{-0.09}$ and
$k_{Z\gamma} = 1.05^{+0.03}_{-0.03}$ as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:nf}. 

The most recent ATLAS combination~\cite{ATLAS:2024hpd} reports an observed (expected) 
95\% confidence level (CL) upper limit on the branching ratio 
$\mathcal{B}(H_{125} \rightarrow \gamma\gamma_D)$ of 1.3\% (1.5\%), shown in Figure~\ref{fig:limit}.
Using the optimized selection strategy described in this work, the expected 95\% CL upper limit is reduced to $0.78^{+0.31}_{-0.21}\,\%$, corresponding to an improvement of approximately 67\% in the excluded branching ratio.
This reduction reflects the enhanced background rejection achieved by the 
additional selection requirements while maintaining signal efficiency. 

In addition, Fig.~\ref{fig:cut-all} shows the histogram distributions of each additional selection variable after the optimized cuts, and Fig.~\ref{fig:n1-all} shows N-1 plots, where all other additional selection variable cuts are applied except the variable in each plot. One can verify that the selections reject most of the background in the N-1 plots.

\subsection{Machine Learning approach}
\subsubsection{Boosted Decision Tree (BDT) training and results}
\label{sec:bdt}

To improve the separation of signal and background beyond cut-based selections, a multivariate boosted decision tree (BDT) classifier was trained using gradient boosting~\cite {FriedmanGBDT,TMVA2007,scikit-learn}. Training is performed after preselections and object definitions. The input variables were selected based on Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves, which indicated their discriminative power for cut points. If the area under the curve (AUC) of the ROC curve is close to 0.5, then the variable is excluded from the list for ML input. To ensure the ML is not biased towards the wrong vertices, $\mathrm{BDT}^{\text{vertex}}_{\text{score}} > 0.1$ is added in the preselections.
The input variables used in the BDT training are 
$\mathrm{BDT}^{\text{vertex}}_{\text{score}}$
$\Delta E_T^{\text{miss}}$, 
$\Delta\phi(j_1,j_2)$, 
$\Delta\phi(E_T^{\text{miss}}, j_{\text{central}})$, 
$\Delta\phi(E_T^{\text{miss}}, \gamma)$, 
$p_{T,j,\text{central}}^{(2)}$, 
$E_T^{\text{miss}}$ significance, 
balance, 
$\eta_\gamma$, 
and $p_T^\gamma$.

\paragraph{Input validation and correlations.}
To ensure that the training is not dominated by redundant information, correlations among the input variables are inspected using the Pearson correlation matrix (Fig.~\ref{fig:bdt:corr}). Moderate correlations are expected among $E_{T}^{miss}$-related observables.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/bdt/correlation_matrix.png}
  \caption{Correlation matrix of the BDT input variables after the baseline selection. The absolute value of the correlation above 0.75 will drop one of the two variables based on their physical significance and their AUC of ROC.}
  \label{fig:bdt:corr}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Training strategy and hyperparameter scan.}
The BDT is trained with gradient boosting, and a randomized hyperparameter scan is used to assess stability and optimize performance~\cite{FriedmanGBDT,scikit-learn}. The scan explores variations in tree depth, learning rate, number of estimators, subsampling, and regularization. I performed a randomized sweep of 100 BDT runs to balance performance and training stability. The parameter distribution for max depth ranged from 3 to 10; the learning rate was drawn from a uniform distribution between 0.001 and 0.1; the number of estimators was a randomized integer between 100 and 900; the subsample was sampled from a uniform distribution between 0.8 and 0.2; the gamma was drawn from between 0.1 and 1.

\subsubsection{Neural Network (NN) training and results}
\label{sec:nn}

In addition to the BDT study, a fully connected Deep Neural Network (DNN) classifier was trained to compare performance. DNN uses the same set of input variables as BDT. Since DNN is more sensitive to biases than BDT, preprocessing for the input variable is needed to prevent undefined or extreme data from disrupting the model. 

For DNN preprocessing, the signal weight is normalized by the background weight to prevent a disproportionate loss function due to uneven weighting, thereby slowing overall learning. In basic cuts, the number of central jets less than 3 is imposed, so there are some events with 0 or 1 jet, resulting in variables, like $\Delta\phi(j_1,j_2)$, $\Delta\phi(E_T^{\text{miss}}, j_{\text{central}})$, and balance, have empty values in events. An additional column was added for each missing-value variable, with 1 indicating a missing value and 0 for the rest.

The model is presented in the table~\ref{tab:nn_model}. To optimize the model with the chosen hyperparameters (number of neurons per layer, learning rate, batch size, and dropout rate), a Bayesian hyperparameter sweep with 50 runs was performed and summarized in Table~\ref{tab:nn_sweep}. Unlike the randomized sweep used in BDT, the Bayesian sweep evaluates previous performance and gradually converges to the best model. Since the number of layers was also undetermined, we started with a 5-layer NN model, with layers 4 and 5 optionally containing zero neurons, allowing analysis of 3- and 4-layer NN models. The neurons are reduced with layers, forcing the model to condense into the most important features of a dark photon signal event. After each layer, the model uses a ReLU activation function for nonlinearity and a dropout rate of 10\%-50\% to prevent overtraining. Finally, a sigmoid activation function reduces each event to a single score between 0 and 1, representing the probability that it is a signal event. To address underfitting and overfitting, an early stopping criterion of 5 epochs was implemented: training stops if the model does not improve for the next 5 epochs.

\section{Conclusions and Outlook}


\begin{table}[htbp]

In this thesis, a comprehensive search for dark photons in Higgs boson decays ($H \to \gamma\gamma_d$) is performed. For the search, four analyses have been set up, each using a different method to enhance the signal-to-background ratio: a cut-based analysis with Nominal as well as Optimized selection criteria, and two machine-learning-based analyses, using either a Boosted Decision Trees (BDT) or a Deep Neural Networks (DNN) to assign a weight to the event based on its kinematics. The performance of these methods is benchmarked using expected upper limits on the branching ratio, expected statistical significance, and the product of significance and signal acceptance.

From the comparison of these four values, we found that machine learning techniques significantly improve analysis compared with traditional cut-and-count methods. The transition from the nominal cuts to the optimally selected sample of events improved the expected significance from $2.475\sigma$ to $2.701\sigma$ and the expected upper limit on the branching ratio of $H \to \gamma\gamma_d$ from $0.86\%$ to $0.78\%$. The DNN method achieved the best expected limit, although BDT has better significance than DNN (the difference is small). The DNN analysis achieved an expected significance of $2.915\sigma$ and expected upper limit of $(0.75^{+0.30}_{-0.22})\,\%$. After optimizing the selection criteria, the analysis improved the expected upper limit on the branching ratio of this process by $70\%$ (from $1.3$ to $0.75$) compared to the previous work. 

The future outlook is to project all three methods to the fully integrated 
luminosity of the LHC Run 3 dataset (approximately $300\text{ fb}^{-1}$) 
and to study the combined Run 3 and Run 2 data to expand the search for 
dark photons at the LHC.
