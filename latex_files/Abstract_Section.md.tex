This thesis presents two independent studies: a GPU-based strip-clustering algorithm designed for the high-luminosity environment of the ATLAS experiment at the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) and an optimization of an ATLAS Run 3 search for Higgs boson decays to dark photons.

The first study focuses on the development and validation strip-detector clustering algorithm in \testtt{Traccc}, a GPU-accelerated tracking toolkit (usable for both offline track reconstruction and the online trigger system) designed to outperform traditional CPU-based algorithms. To support more data processing in a higher-collision-per-second environment during the Phase-II upgrade program for the High-Luminosity LHC (HL-LHC), we propose Event Filter (EF) Tracking GPU pipelines that use GPU-based algorithms. The strip clusterization algorithm groups cell hits from silicon strip sensors to form measurements and is integrated into the EF Tracking GPU pipelines in the \texttt{ATHENA} framework. The algorithm is validated and shows good consistency in comparing differences in local measurement positions between \texttt{TRACCC} and \texttt{ATHENA}. 

Alongside this detector-focused work, there is a contribution to a search for Higgs boson decays to photons + dark photons produced via gluon-gluon fusion. The search is based on an integrated luminosity of $135~\mathrm{fb}^{-1}$ of ATLAS Run3 data; we also provide a projection to the full Run 3 dataset of xx $\mathrm{fb}^{-1}$. Motivated by finding a potential candidate for dark matter, we search for events with the final state of a Standard Model (SM) photon, missing transverse energy ($E_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{miss}}$), 
and collimated sprays of hadrons called jets. My analysis aims to maximize the signal-to-background significance by optimizing selection criteria based on distributions of various variables. A Machine Learning (ML) classifier study, including Boosted Decision Tree (BDT) and Neural Network (NN) approaches, was also developed to further enhance signal sensitivity.
